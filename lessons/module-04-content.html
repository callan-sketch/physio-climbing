Assessing & Diagnosing Common Climbing Injuries

~18 min read Climbing Injury Prevention & Management

4.1 Purpose of This Module

Modules 2 and 3 built the anatomical, biomechanical, and epidemiological foundation for climbing injury. This module puts that foundation to clinical use: a site-by-site framework for assessing and differentiating the injuries most likely to present in a climbing patient, structured around six regions — fingers, wrist, elbow, shoulder, knee, and ankle/foot. Each section pairs the climbing-specific mechanism with the physical examination findings and imaging pathway that help distinguish between the realistic differentials for that site. This is the direct clinical bridge into Module 5's treatment and rehabilitation content.

4.A Fingers & Hand Injuries

Pulley Injury: Grading and Assessment in Detail

The bowstringing test remains the most widely cited clinical sign of pulley rupture, performed with the finger's PIP joint at approximately 20° and DIP at approximately 40° of flexion while the examiner applies resistance against the fingertip (the "counter-flexion" technique) — increasing resistance increases any bowstring effect, making it easier to detect. It is worth noting, however, that systematic review evidence has not established formal sensitivity or specificity values for this test in isolation, so clinical findings should be interpreted alongside imaging rather than used to rule pulley injury in or out on their own.

GradeInjuryTypical Management
IPulley strain — no tendon-bone dehiscence.Conservative: taping, activity modification.
IIComplete A4 rupture, or partial A2/A3 rupture.Conservative: taping, graded reloading.
IIIComplete rupture of A2 or A3.Conservative: thermoplastic ring + taping.
IVMultiple pulley rupture, or single rupture with lumbrical or collateral ligament involvement.Surgical reconstruction generally indicated.
Clinical note

Dynamic ultrasound is considered the reference standard for diagnosing pulley rupture and grading severity, since it allows real-time assessment of tendon-to-bone distance during resisted flexion. A healthy pulley results in a tendon to bone distance of 2mm, a torn one 4–6mm; MRI is a useful second-line study when ultrasound is inconclusive or when associated structures (lumbrical, collateral ligaments) need clearer visualisation.

Hand Injuries in Climbers

Although flexor pulley injuries receive the greatest attention in climbing medicine, several other structures within the hand are frequently injured. These conditions often present with symptoms that mimic pulley injuries, making accurate assessment essential. Careful history taking, knowledge of the mechanism of injury, and a thorough clinical examination are often sufficient to distinguish these injuries and guide appropriate management.

The intrinsic muscles of the hand, collateral ligaments, joint capsules, digital nerves and flexor tendon sheath can all become overloaded during climbing. These injuries are particularly common during dynamic movements, two- and three-finger pocket climbing, aggressive crimping, gastons and twisting movements where individual fingers are subjected to different loads. Although many of these injuries respond well to conservative management, delayed diagnosis can prolong recovery and contribute to persistent pain or reduced grip performance.

Common Hand Injury in Climbers: Lumbrical Strain (Lumbrical Shift Syndrome)

Lumbrical injuries are among the more distinctive soft tissue injuries encountered in climbers. Unlike most muscles, the lumbricals originate from the flexor digitorum profundus tendons rather than directly from bone, before inserting into the extensor mechanism on the back of the fingers. This unique anatomy allows them to coordinate finger movement and contribute to fine motor control, but also makes them vulnerable when adjacent fingers are loaded in different positions.

The classic injury mechanism occurs during climbing on two- or three-finger pockets. While one finger remains deeply flexed inside the pocket, another may become relatively extended or unloaded. This creates tension between adjacent flexor tendons, placing a shearing force across the lumbrical muscle. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as the Quadriga effect, is unique to the anatomy of the hand and explains why lumbrical injuries are seen far more frequently in climbers than in most other athletes.

A second, equally common mechanism — sometimes referred to informally as the "pinkie drop" — occurs during the three-finger drag grip, where the little finger is deliberately kept off the hold while the middle three fingers bear load. Because the little finger's flexor tendon stays relatively slack while its neighbours are fully loaded, the same relative-extension shearing mechanism responsible for pocket-related strain occurs here too, independent of pocket use entirely. This has a direct rehabilitation implication: where the three-finger drag is the primary or contributing mechanism, return-to-climbing progressions need to specifically reintroduce and load that exact grip pattern in a controlled, graded way, rather than assuming general open-hand reloading is sufficient.

Patients typically report pain deep within the palm, most commonly at the base of the ring finger, rather than directly over the finger joints or pulleys. Symptoms may initially present as a dull ache but become sharp when the injured finger is loaded independently or when pocket climbing is attempted again.

Clinical Assessment

Several examination findings help distinguish a lumbrical injury from pulley or flexor tendon pathology.

  • Pain is located deep within the palm rather than over the finger.
  • Symptoms increase when one finger is passively extended while adjacent fingers remain flexed.
  • Pocket climbing is significantly more painful than open-hand gripping.
  • Direct palpation between the flexor tendons reproduces symptoms.
  • Commonly associated with FDP strains and co-existing forearm pain on palpation and contraction.

Because pulley injuries, flexor tendon strains and collateral ligament injuries may produce similar symptoms, clinicians should always consider these differential diagnoses during assessment.

Management Principles

Most lumbrical injuries respond well to conservative management. Early treatment focuses on reducing excessive loading while maintaining finger mobility. Climbers should temporarily avoid pocket climbing and any movement that reproduces sharp pain, but complete immobilisation is rarely required.

As symptoms improve, rehabilitation progresses towards restoring intrinsic hand strength, independent finger control and gradual reloading through open-hand positions before returning to pockets. Progressive exposure to climbing-specific loading remains the most effective strategy for returning athletes safely to performance.

A simple technique-correction point is worth passing on to coaches and climbers alike as prevention: actively keeping the proximal phalanx of the little finger adjacent to the ring finger — rather than allowing it to drift — reduces the relative shearing force between the two flexor tendons that underlies this injury. This is an easily coached strategy worth introducing alongside any rehabilitation programme, particularly for climbers who habitually favour pocket use or the three-finger drag.

Clinical note

Climbers often describe any finger pain as a "pulley injury." Pain located in the palm rather than over the finger, especially after climbing on two- or three-finger pockets, should immediately raise suspicion of a lumbrical strain. Accurate diagnosis prevents unnecessary pulley rehabilitation and allows a more targeted return-to-climbing programme.

Clinical note

Provocative wrist tests — including those for TFCC pathology — have consistently shown limited diagnostic accuracy in isolation in the physiotherapy literature. Use a cluster of findings (mechanism, palpation, and provocation tests together) to build clinical suspicion, and correlate with imaging before committing to a specific diagnosis, particularly for TFCC and hamate pathology where presentations can be diffuse and easy to miss.

Dorsal wrist impingement — pain on wrist extension, often provoked by mantling moves or crimping with the wrist extended — is a further differential worth considering alongside TFCC pathology, since both can present with dorsal or dorsoulnar-sided pain on loaded extension.

4.C Elbow

Elbow pain in climbers is most frequently medial, reflecting the combined demands of sustained gripping and the pronated pulling position required on the wall. Lateral-sided presentations are less common but remain an important differential, particularly when a presumed epicondylalgia fails to respond to appropriate treatment.

4.D Shoulder

Shoulder pain is disproportionately common in more experienced and higher-grade climbers, and presents across a recognisable set of overuse and, less frequently, acute presentations. A standard shoulder examination battery adapted for climbers typically includes:

  • Active and passive range of motion, including glenohumeral internal/external rotation.
  • Scapular kinematics and stability: clinical observation of scapulohumeral rhythm during active elevation, assessing for deficient posterior tilt or upward rotation, alongside the upper trapezius dominance and serratus anterior/lower trapezius underactivity described in Module 3.
  • Instability testing (apprehension test) given the dynamic, sometimes forceful overhead loading involved in climbing moves.
  • Rotator cuff assessment: Jobe's test (empty can), external rotation strength, lift-off test, and lag signs.
  • Subacromial impingement: Neer's test and the modified Hawkins-Kennedy test.
  • Long biceps tendon and superior labral assessment: O'Brien's test, alongside a general awareness that no single clinical test reliably confirms a SLAP lesion in isolation — a cluster of positive findings raises suspicion rather than any one test confirming the diagnosis.
  • Acromioclavicular joint: local tenderness and the cross-body adduction test.

Whenever feasible, clinical assessment should be augmented by video analysis of the athlete in motion on the wall, as this often reveals compensatory patterns that clinic-based manual muscle testing may miss. Scapular dyskinesis and excessive recruitment of the rhomboids or upper trapezius are frequently more apparent during loaded, dynamic tasks, providing a clearer diagnostic bridge between table-based findings and actual climbing performance.

Clinical note

MRI studies of experienced climbers have found a very high background rate of labral, biceps tendon, and cartilage abnormalities even in shoulders with no current symptoms — in one cohort, every climber studied had multiple MRI abnormalities, yet only a minority of shoulders were symptomatic at the time of scanning. This means imaging findings in a climber's shoulder should be interpreted cautiously and correlated closely with the clinical picture, rather than assumed to explain current symptoms on their own.

4.E Knee

Knee injuries in climbers cluster around four recognisable mechanisms — high step, drop knee, heel hook, and ground fall — each with a reasonably distinct injury profile, reflecting the rotational and shear forces described in Module 3.

Technique / MechanismTypical Structure(s) at RiskKey Assessment Points
High stepMedial meniscus — knee fully flexed and weight-bearing while the tibia/femur rotate.Joint-line tenderness; McMurray's test; assess for effusion.
Drop kneeMedial meniscus and MCL, via a similar high-flexion rotational load.As above; also check for pain on passive internal/external rotation at 90° flexion as well as on a valgus stress test and a McMurray with an abduction/external rotation component.
Heel hookIliotibial band (sprain occurs almost exclusively via this mechanism), biceps femoris (hamstring), lateral collateral ligament, popliteus, lateral meniscus, posterior cruciate ligament.Lateral knee palpation, Ober's test for ITB; resisted knee flexion and posterolateral thigh palpation for hamstring; posterior drawer for PCL if hyperflexion/hyperextension mechanism reported.
Ground fallACL, multiligament knee injury, complex/bilateral meniscal tears — higher-energy, less climbing-technique-specific pattern.Lachman's test, posterior drawer, varus/valgus stress testing; have a lower threshold for onward imaging given the higher-energy mechanism.

Ground-fall knee injuries warrant particular caution: evidence from bouldering-specific case series suggests these tend to be higher-energy and more structurally complex than technique-related injuries, with a substantial proportion involving multiple structures (meniscus, ligaments) simultaneously rather than an isolated single-structure injury.

Worth a brief mention alongside the heel hook mechanism: "rocking" onto a high heel hook drives the hip into a combination of deep flexion and external rotation — a position pattern already associated with femoroacetabular impingement and labral irritation in athletes more broadly. This hasn't been specifically studied in climbers, but the mechanical logic is directly analogous to the high step position already discussed above, and hip pain accompanying or following a heel-hook-heavy session is worth considering alongside the knee-focused differentials in the table.

4.F Ankle & Foot

The foot and ankle sit at an unusual intersection in climbing medicine: acute, high-energy trauma from ground falls at one end, and chronic, footwear-driven overuse deformity at the other. Both categories are common enough that it is worth screening for each specifically, since the two rarely present in quite the same way.

PresentationMechanismAssessment Notes
Lateral ankle sprainTight, downturned shoes hold the foot in a relatively supinated position; a loss of purchase during smearing or edging can drive the ankle into inversion.Anterior drawer and talar tilt tests; recurrent instability is common in climbers and worth asking about specifically.
Forefoot/toe overuse (hallux valgus, hammer/claw toes, subungual haematoma)Chronic compression from tight, downturned, asymmetric climbing shoes concentrating pressure on the great toe.Visual inspection of foot shape and nails; ask about typical shoe size relative to street shoe size.
Retrocalcaneal bursitis / Achilles tendinopathy / Haglund's deformityHeel counter pressure from tight-fitting shoes, aggravated by heel-hooking.Palpate posterior heel; pain with toe-off or slab-climbing positions.
Calcaneus / talus fracture, osteochondral talar lesionGround falls — acute, high-energy axial loading through the heel.Any fall-related heel/ankle pain with inability to weight-bear warrants imaging; osteochondral lesions may present more insidiously after an initial fall.
Clinical note

Foot and ankle injuries are reported to account for close to half of all acute climbing injuries in some cohorts, yet receive far less attention in the climbing-medicine literature and in athlete self-reporting than upper-limb injuries. Actively screening climbing patients for foot and ankle symptoms — rather than waiting for them to be volunteered — is a reasonable practice given this reporting gap.

Summary

A structured, site-by-site assessment approach — grounded in the climbing-specific mechanisms established in Modules 2 and 3 — allows the realistic differentials at each region to be narrowed efficiently: pulley injury, tenosynovitis, and capsulitis in the fingers; TFCC, hamate, and De Quervain's pathology at the wrist; medial and lateral elbow tendinopathy; the shoulder's overuse-dominant pattern; the knee's technique-specific mechanisms; and the ankle/foot's dual acute-versus-overuse picture. With diagnosis established, Module 5 turns to evidence-based treatment and rehabilitation progressions for each of these presentations.
